Before anything else

What this is, and what it is not

What it is

A methodological result about how risk estimates respond to distributional assumptions. Demonstrated on a 9-bus representation of Oʻahu and on the standard IEEE-14 test network.

What it is not

A study of your system. Our topology is a sketch we constructed; our line ratings are assumed. No contingency analysis, no forced outages, no reserves, no unit commitment, no storage.

So we are deliberately not quoting you a reserve margin

Our model produces one. We are leaving it out, because a sparse 9-bus sketch has far fewer delivery paths than a real meshed network — which biases that number pessimistically by an amount we cannot size. Presenting it would be exactly the error this talk is about.

The distribution is the risk
Credibility slide — do not rush itPutting the limits up front, before any result, is what earns the right to the rest. If asked about the reserve margin number: it exists, we can share the methodology, but the topology it rests on is not good enough to quote to this room. That answer is the point of the talk.

The experiment

Hold the grid completely fixed. Re-roll only demand.

Fixed: topology, generator capacities, marginal costs, line ratings, reference bus. Nothing about the network moves between scenarios.

Rolled: the demand at every node, redrawn 10,000 times.

Then read the answer as a distribution, not a number.

Method, part 1Emphasise the control: because the network never changes, any difference between runs is attributable to the demand model alone. That is what makes the comparison clean.

The experiment

We never let the solver say “infeasible”

A dispatch model that cannot serve demand normally returns an error. An error is one bit of information: yes or no. We wanted megawatts.

What we did

Added load shedding as a priced decision, at a value of lost load of $10,000/MWh. The model may always refuse an order — it simply pays dearly for it.

Why it matters to you

Every scenario now returns an answer, and “infeasible” becomes a measurable quantity: how many megawatts short, and where. Shortfall becomes something you can rank, budget against and compare.

The distribution is the risk
Method, part 2This is the one modelling choice worth defending in detail. It is standard in the literature but often skipped in practice, and it is why we can report a distribution of shortfall instead of a pass/fail count.

The assumption under test

The demand model we were told to use

Draw each node's demand from an exponential distribution scaled to its average. It is a common default: analytically convenient, widely used, and it requires only a mean.

What that quietly asserts

That a neighbourhood's most likely demand is zero, and that its tail runs past four times its own average. Its coefficient of variation is exactly 1.

Nobody writes that down

It arrives as a line of code, not as a claim about the island. That is what makes it dangerous: it is an assumption that never has to be defended, because it is never stated.

The distribution is the risk
Set up the villainBe fair to the assumption: it is not stupid, it is convenient and it is standard when you have nothing but a mean. The problem is that on Oahu we DO have more than a mean.

The consequence

Under the assumed model the island exceeds twice its average demand about one hour in nineteen. In 8,760 recorded hours the real island did it zero times — its busiest hour ever does not reach 1.5× average.

ACT III

Why the two risks behave differently

The distribution is the risk
TransitionNow the mechanism. The next two slides are the intellectual contribution: congestion and adequacy are different KINDS of quantity, and that is why one assumption moves them in opposite directions.

Mechanism

Two questions, two different geometries

Congestion asks “how much”

Whether a line fills up depends almost entirely on the island total — and hardly at all on which neighbourhoods are asking.

One threshold governs it.

Adequacy asks “where”

Whether the island can physically serve demand depends heavily on the mix of neighbourhoods. Turn the same total toward a different set of places and the answer changes completely.

It has a different limit in every direction.

Why that produces opposite movement

The assumed model gets the total wrong — it spends far too much time idling low — so it understates congestion. It also aims in random directions, and most directions are worse than the one real demand actually takes — so it overstates blackouts.

The distribution is the risk
The ideaSay it plainly: congestion is about magnitude, adequacy is about direction. We initially conjectured the opposite for congestion and our own data refuted us — worth admitting out loud, it plays well and it is true.

Mechanism · the strongest evidence

One number predicts congestion under both models

585

megawatts. A single island-wide total threshold, fitted once. Above it, a line is at its limit; below it, none is.

Congestion frequencyFrom 585 MW aloneActually observedGap
Assumed Exp(1)59.0%58.40%0.6 pp
Real filed hours92.2%92.51%0.3 pp

Two demand distributions with nothing in common, and one scalar reproduces both congestion rates to under a percentage point. The reason the rates differ is simply that the assumed model idles below 585 MW 41% of the time, while the real island does so in only 7.8% of its 8,760 hours.

The distribution is the risk
Spend time hereThis is the most defensible technical claim in the deck. Two radically different distributions, one scalar, sub-percentage-point accuracy on both. It also means congestion risk can be screened cheaply without running the full model — which is a practical takeaway, not just an academic one.

Why the blackout risk was imaginary

For adequacy there is no single threshold. There is a different limit for every possible mix of neighbourhoods. We measured that limit across many mixes:

Real demand does not wander. It takes one habitual shape, day after day, and that shape happens to be a comparatively favourable one. A model that aims randomly finds trouble that the island, on its actual daily pattern, never meets.

The counterpartImportant caveat if pressed: "lucky" is measured on OUR topology. The mechanism — that real demand occupies one narrow region of the space while the assumption explores all of it — is general and does not depend on our network being right.

A third example

The same trap, on the supply side

Adding up what generators are rated to produce is not the same as what the network can deliver to where people are. On our model the gap is 28%.

Why we raise it

Because it is the same error in a different costume: a headline number whose assumptions are buried. A reserve margin computed against nameplate and one computed against deliverable capacity are not the same statistic.

Why we are not giving you our number

The gap depends entirely on topology and line ratings — exactly what our 9-bus sketch gets least right, and in a direction that makes things look worse than they are. Our figure would be precise and wrong.

The transferable point: ask what capacity figure a reserve margin was computed against, and whether the delivery network was in the calculation at all.

The distribution is the risk
Restraint as credibilityDeclining to give a number you have is a strong move in this room. If someone pushes, offer the methodology and the code, not the figure. That is the honest position and it reads as confidence rather than evasion.

The takeaway

What to ask of any grid study on your desk

QuestionWhy it decides the headline number
What distribution was demand drawn from, and why that one?It sets the answer. Ours moved shedding from 19.77% to zero.
Was it fitted to filed load data, or assumed?A mean is not a distribution. Shape is where the risk lives.
Does the study report congestion and adequacy separately?They respond to assumptions in opposite directions. One aggregate number hides that.
What capacity was the reserve margin computed against?Nameplate and deliverable are different statistics.

None of these require re-running anyone's model. They are answerable from the methodology section — and they are the questions that determine whether the headline figure means anything.

The structural catch

Every item on both tiers needs chronological time. Outages persist. Batteries carry charge. Ramps connect one hour to the next.

Our design samples hours independently. Adding these is not bolting on constraints — it is rebuilding the study as a time-series simulation.

One measurement that shows why it matters

Real Oʻahu load moves an average of 41.9 MW hour to hour, and never more than 138. Chain the assumed model's draws together and the average swing is 424 MW — ten times larger. Ramping would have been a third place where the two models diverge.

The distribution is the risk
Tier twoPoint 9 is worth saying explicitly: on Oahu today the bigger uncertainty is the supply side, not demand. Our paper randomises demand and treats supply as firm, which is backwards for this island in 2026. Say so — they will already know it, and naming it first is better than being told.

In closing

The assumption you cannot see is the one setting the number.

What we showed

One line of code — the demand distribution — moved estimated shedding from 19.77% to zero while moving congestion from 58.4% to 92.5%. Same grid, same solver, no error.

What it means for reading studies

Misspecification does not simply inflate risk. It relocates it — which means a study can be conservative and dangerously misleading at the same time.
