The question

Our earlier study said the shape of demand decides the risk.

But that study let every power plant change output freely, with no limits. Real plants cannot do this.

So we ask one question

Does that finding survive when the machines are real?

What the earlier study assumed

A power plant was one line of maths.

0  ≤  P  ≤  Pmax

Output can be anything between zero and maximum. It can change by any amount, in any hour, at no cost.

Output How much electricity the plant makes right now, in megawatts (MW).
What a real machine has

A real power plant has three limits. Think of a heavy truck.

1

It cannot go too slow

When the plant is switched on, it must make at least a minimum amount. Below that, the machine is unstable.

MINIMUM OUTPUT
2

It cannot change too fast

Output can only move so much from one hour to the next. A truck cannot jump from 20 to 100 km/h instantly.

RAMP LIMIT
3

It remembers

Once started, it must stay on for several hours. Once stopped, it must stay off. Starting costs money.

MINIMUM RUN TIME
Assumed

All three values are chosen by us, by technology type. No public form reports them for these machines.

Limit 1  ·  minimum output

Switched on means working hard.

An oil steam plant cannot idle gently. If it is on, it must make at least 40% of its maximum. There is no setting below that.

This matters at midday, when rooftop solar is strong and the island needs very little from the plants. The plants still cannot go quiet.

Oil steam A large, old type of plant that boils oil to make steam. Half of Oʻahu's units are this type.
Limit 2  ·  ramp limit

Output moves slowly.

A steam unit can change its output by about 30% of its maximum in one hour. No faster.

So the plant cannot answer a sudden jump in demand. It must have started climbing an hour earlier.

This is the limit that creates the new risk we found. Keep it in mind.

Limit 3  ·  minimum run time

A decision made now controls the next several hours.

This is the limit that makes the problem hard. The model cannot choose each hour separately. It must plan ahead.

8 h
An oil steam unit, once started, must run at least eight hours
24 h
The rubbish-burning plant runs almost continuously — the island's waste keeps arriving
1 h
Small diesel engines are flexible. They start and stop freely

Why this bites. A unit needed for the evening peak, which cannot switch off for eight hours, must keep running through the quiet middle of the day — at its minimum output, burning fuel, while the sun does the work.

Honesty, early

The wires are our invention.

The power plants are real and filed. The people are real and counted. But the network joining the nine points is a simple sketch we designed.

Hawaiian Electric describes two corridors, one north and one south, on a public web page. We built ten lines from that description.

Because of this, any number we give about congestion is a statement about our sketch — not about the real grid. The direction is meaningful. The size is not.

The method

We add one new rule at a time.

If we changed several things at once and a number moved, we would not know which change moved it. So we climb a ladder, one step per rule.

STEP 1  LP

Output between zero and maximum. Nothing else. This is what the earlier study could express.

STEP 2  +PMIN

Add: a unit that is on must make at least its minimum. Now the model must also decide on or off.

STEP 3  +RAMP

Add: output can only change so much from one hour to the next.

STEP 4  +FULL

Add: minimum run times, and a cost every time a unit starts.

Why the whole experiment had to be rebuilt

A ramp limit needs a yesterday.

The earlier study picked hours at random. Hour 5,000 could follow hour 12. There was no "previous hour" for a ramp limit to hold on to.

So Set 9 walks the year in real order — day by day, carrying forward which units are on, how long they have been on, and at what output.

Why it matters This is a different experiment, not a small repair. That is why Set 9 produces all of its own numbers and reuses none of the old ones.

We show our mistakes  ·  1 of 2
Error

Put those two numbers together.

Every oil steam unit has a minimum output of 40% and can only change by 30% in an hour.

To start, it must go from 0 to 40 in one hour. But it may only climb 30.

So it can never start. Ever.

Half the island's fleet was frozen off, permanently, by a rule we wrote ourselves.

What the broken model reported
95.8%

of all hours in the year could not be supplied.

If we had believed this number, the conclusion would have been that Oʻahu is on the edge of collapse. It is not. The number was produced entirely by our own modelling mistake.

How we caught it

The error message was fine. The economics were absurd.

What the model was doing

  • Cheap oil steam sitting idle, hour after hour
  • Expensive small diesel engines running day and night
  • The island "failing" while 43% of its capacity was unused

No sensible operator would ever dispatch like this. That was the clue — not a crash, not a warning.

The fix

Starting up is a separate movement from normal running. A unit is allowed a bigger step on the hour it starts, exactly enough to reach its minimum. This is standard practice; we had simply missed it.

Why show you this? Because a team that reports the mistake it caught is telling you the checking actually happened. There is a second one coming.

Now the harder half of the problem

We know what the whole island used. We do not know where.

The filing gives one number for every hour of the year: total demand for Oʻahu. 8,760 numbers.

It does not say how much was used in Honolulu, and how much in Waianae. Nobody publishes that.

The problem hiding in the old method

One lever moved all nine places.

The old method gave each place a fixed share of the island total, based on how many people live there. That share never changed.

So when demand rose, all nine rose together by the same proportion. When it fell, all nine fell together. The balance between places could never change.

Our whole research question is about where demand sits, not just how much there is. And that was the one thing we had frozen.

What changed when we did that

The city was badly under-counted.

Honolulu's share of the island's electricity rises by 17 percentage points.

Ewa-West rises too — and it has very few people. That is Campbell Industrial Park: the refineries and the port. It holds 7.8% of the island's industrial jobs with 3.0% of its population.

This is the check that the method works. It does not simply push everything into the city.

But we had not solved the real problem

Better shares. Still frozen.

The new shares were more accurate — but they were still one fixed set of numbers, used in all 8,760 hours. The balance between places still could not move.

To make the balance move, we needed something new: a clock for each kind of demand. Offices are busy at midday. Homes are busy in the evening. Industry runs flat. Separate those, and the island's demand starts to rotate on its own.

Before we show you results

We went looking for our own mistakes.

We found two. Both were in the new demand data. Both are now fixed, and both changed the answer.

Error 1

The calendar was two days out

Error 2

We counted people, not electricity

Error 1  ·  cause and repair

Dates carry over between years. Weekdays do not.

The simulated buildings run on a calendar year whose 1 January is a Sunday. Our year, 2021, begins on a Friday.

Both years have 365 days, so every date lines up perfectly. We assumed the day names did too. They are two days apart.

Confirmed independently against three different building types — an office, a school and a hospital. All three give the same answer: shift by two days.

Good news and bad news. Our headline result comes from the shape of the day, which barely moved. But the working week was backwards, and that is now corrected.

Error 2  ·  a deeper one
Error

We were counting people. We should have been counting electricity.

To blend the daily shapes of offices, hotels, shops and hospitals together, we weighted each one by how many people work there.

That quietly assumes an office worker and a hotel worker stand for the same number of kilowatts. They do not.

Finding 1 of 2  ·  the reassurance

The earlier finding survives.

The gap between assuming demand is random and using the real filed record did not disappear when we made the machines real. It held at every one of the four levels.

This matters because it means the earlier result was not an accident of a simple model. If you choose the wrong statistical assumption about demand, you will compute the wrong risk — no matter how carefully you model the machines.

Finding 2 of 2  ·  the new risk

A shortage of speed,
not of size.

On about 53 hours a year, real demand moves faster than the committed fleet can follow. The machines exist. They cannot get there in time.

The earlier model was completely blind to this, because it had no concept of one hour following another.

MWh not supplied across the whole year

It is small. 33.8 MWh on an island averaging 744 MW. It is not zero, and a battery is exactly the device that removes it. That is the obvious next study.

One result we deliberately do not claim

Congestion moves a lot. Do not quote the size.

"Hours with a full line" swings between 58.9% and 94.8% as we climb the ladder. That is a very large movement.

But remember slide 13: every electrical value in our network is assumed. The line limits, the lengths, the resistances — all ours.

So this number describes our sketch of the grid, not the grid. The direction is meaningful and robust. The magnitude is not a claim about Oʻahu.

What this model still cannot tell you

Four gaps. The first is the largest.

Military load is missing

Our jobs data excludes uniformed military. Pearl Harbor–Hickam, Schofield and Kāneʻohe are large loads that run around the clock — and they contribute nothing to our daily shapes. Schofield has its own power station in this fleet precisely to serve that load.

No machine ever breaks

Every unit is assumed available every hour of the year. For a study about whether supply is adequate, this is the biggest single gap.

No reserve for the largest failure

On an island with no cable to anywhere, surviving the sudden loss of the biggest unit is the real planning test. We do not model it.

No batteries, and simplified physics

No storage, no reserves. The power flow model ignores voltage, reactive power and losses.

What would improve this most

Three things, in order of value.

1

Real operating limits for these 24 machines

Minimum output, ramp rate, minimum run times. These are the numbers our second finding rests on entirely, and every one of them is currently our guess.

2

A real network model

Actual line ratings and impedances would turn every congestion number from a description of our sketch into a statement about Oʻahu.

3

Military load, and forced outages

The two gaps most likely to change the answer rather than refine it.

In closing

Three sentences.

One. Modelling the plants as real machines did not overturn our earlier finding. The assumption you make about demand still decides the risk you compute.

Two. A new risk appeared that the earlier model could not see: about 53 hours a year where demand moves faster than the fleet can follow. It is small. It is not zero. A battery removes it.

Three. The network in this model is ours, not Hawaiian Electric's. Trust the directions. Do not quote the congestion numbers as facts about the island.
