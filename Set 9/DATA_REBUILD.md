# Set 9 — rebuilt demand data

Everything below is generated by two scripts, both of which download their own
sources: `prepare_demand.py` (**v2** — the static rebuild) and
`prepare_hourly_demand.py` (**v3** — the hourly rebuild). No number here was
typed by hand.

## What was wrong

Set 8 and the first Set 9 run split island demand across the nine buses by
**2020 resident population**, and nothing else. The project's own
`SOURCES.md` already named the problem:

> "Honolulu's commercial, hotel, and military load and the rooftop PV already
> embedded in net load both bias its true net import upward relative to that
> share, so the urban import result is conservative."

That was a stated suspicion. It is now a measurement.

## The measurement

Workplace jobs on Oʻahu, from Census LEHD LODES 2021, by judicial district:

| District | Residents | **Jobs** | Gap |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Honolulu | 39.9% | **71.0%** | +31 pp |
| Ewa (both) | 35.5% | 19.9% | −16 pp |
| Koolaupoko | 11.9% | 5.2% | −7 pp |
| Waianae | 5.1% | 0.9% | −4 pp |
| Wahiawa | 4.1% | 1.3% | −3 pp |
| Koolauloa | 2.2% | 1.0% | −1 pp |
| Waialua | 1.3% | 0.7% | −1 pp |

Oʻahu is a commuter island. Allocating load by where people *sleep* moves a
large block of commercial demand out of downtown Honolulu every hour of the
day.

## The rebuild

```
share[bus] = f_res · population[bus]      (Census 2020)
           + f_com · commercial_jobs[bus] (LODES CNS07–CNS20)
           + f_ind · industrial_jobs[bus] (LODES CNS01–CNS06)
```

Sector weights from **EIA Form 861, 2021** retail sales.

**Corrected to Oʻahu only.** v2 originally used statewide Hawaii figures. EIA-861
reports each utility separately, and *Hawaiian Electric Co Inc* is the Oʻahu
entity — Maui Electric and Hawaii Electric Light file their own returns. Its
6.17 TWh of retail sales against 6.52 TWh of measured net load leaves a ~5% gap,
which is what transmission and distribution losses look like — an independent
confirmation that this is the right entity.

| Sector | **Oʻahu only** | Statewide (what v2 first used) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Residential | **26.75%** | 31.61% |
| Commercial | **31.12%** | 31.16% |
| Industrial | **42.13%** | 37.23% |

Oʻahu is more industrial and less residential than the state average — Campbell
Industrial Park, the refineries and the military bases all sit here.

## Limitations — read before quoting

**1. LODES excludes uniformed military.** This is the most serious one for
Oʻahu. Pearl Harbor–Hickam, Schofield Barracks and Kaneohe MCB are large loads
carried by personnel this dataset does not count. Wahiawa (Schofield) and
Koolaupoko (Kaneohe) are therefore likely **under**-weighted in v2 — and note
that Schofield Generating Station, six units in the fleet, exists precisely to
serve that load. v2 cuts Wahiawa from 4.10% to 2.21%; the truth is probably
between the two.

**2. ~~The EIA sector weights are statewide~~ — FIXED.** They are now taken from
*Hawaiian Electric Co Inc* alone, which is the Oʻahu entity. See the corrected
table above.

**3. Jobs proxy commercial load; they do not measure it.** A data centre
employs few people and draws heavily. A call centre is the reverse.

**4. ~~The allocation is still static~~ — FIXED in v3.** v2 holds every bus at a
fixed share in all 8,760 hours, so its demand direction cannot move. That is
what v3 solves; see the section below.

# v3 — hourly demand, and a direction that finally moves

`prepare_hourly_demand.py` produces `data/oahu_bus_hourly_demand.csv`:
**9 buses × 8,760 hours**, with the mix changing every hour.

## Why v1 and v2 were not enough

Both scale a **single fixed share vector**. Their demand matrices are
**rank one** — 78,840 entries carrying only 8,760 pieces of information,
because the nine bus loads can only grow and shrink together. The demand
*direction* never moves. The paper's own central claim is that direction is
what drives adequacy, so the one quantity that matters most was frozen.

## How v3 works

Oʻahu's load is not one thing. It is offices downtown that peak at midday,
houses in the suburbs that peak in the evening, and industry that runs flat.
Those three have **different spatial footprints and different daily shapes**,
so separating them makes the island's demand vector rotate.

```
gross[s,t] = sector s island load in hour t
D[i,t]     = Σ_s a[i,s] · gross[s,t]  −  pv[i] · dgpv[t]
```

| Sector | Spatial key | Hourly shape |
|---|---|---|
| Residential | Census 2020 population | OpenEI Honolulu TMY3 residential profile |
| Commercial | LODES commercial jobs | employment-weighted composite of 15 Honolulu reference buildings |
| Industrial | LODES industrial jobs | **flat** — no industrial reference building exists |
| Rooftop PV | *assumed:* population | the `dgpv` layer, subtracted |

## It works, and for the right reasons

| Check | Result |
|---|---|
| Island total preserved | max error **3×10⁻⁴ MW** |
| Share-matrix rank | **2**, not 1 — singular values 62.73, 6.30, 0.15 |
| Honolulu share range | **51.16% → 85.50%** across the year |
| Weekday vs weekend, Honolulu at noon | **77.0%** vs **71.3%** — the right way round |

And the daily pattern falls out on its own, unforced:

| Hour | Honolulu share | Commercial | Residential | Rooftop PV |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 00:00 | 55.8% | 320 MW | 397 MW | 41 MW |
| 04:00 | 59.5% | 317 MW | 249 MW | 30 MW |
| **12:00** | **75.4%** | 660 MW | 353 MW | **405 MW** |
| 16:00 | 68.6% | 641 MW | 407 MW | 310 MW |
| **22:00** | **53.7%** | 334 MW | **547 MW** | 57 MW |

Downtown dominates at midday — commercial load peaks *and* rooftop solar is
suppressing net load in the residential districts. By late evening the island's
demand has swung back out to where people sleep. Neither pattern was imposed;
both emerge from the sector shapes.

## Rank 2, not 3 — and why that's honest

Industrial is modelled flat, so it contributes no independent time variation.
The second dimension comes entirely from residential-versus-commercial. A real
industrial shape would likely add a third.

## The commercial blend: weighted by energy, not headcount

`commercial_shape()` blends fifteen reference buildings. Each one's weight is

```
weight[building] = mix[building] × jobs[its job category] × kWh_per_worker[its activity]
```

The third factor was added after the first v3 build, which weighted by **headcount
alone**. That silently assumed an office worker and a hotel worker stand for the
same kilowatts. EIA **CBECS 2018** says otherwise — table C22 (electricity by
principal building activity) over table B1 (workers by the same activity):

| Activity | kWh per worker per year |
|---|---:|
| Food sales | 58,888 |
| Lodging | 36,179 |
| Food service | 21,151 |
| Mercantile | 20,266 |
| Inpatient health | 18,071 |
| Warehouse | 13,416 |
| Education | 12,343 |
| Outpatient health | 10,134 |
| **Office** | **6,912** — the least intense of all |

An office worker is the **least** energy-intense worker CBECS measures. Weighting
by headcount therefore over-weighted offices badly:

| Category | By headcount | **By CBECS energy** | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Office | 39.5% | **18.4%** | −21.1 pp |
| Hotel + food | 12.9% | **24.4%** | +11.5 pp |
| Retail | 12.0% | **24.3%** | +12.3 pp |
| Health | 16.2% | 16.3% | +0.1 |
| Schools | 13.6% | 11.3% | −2.3 |
| Warehouse | 5.7% | 5.2% | −0.5 |

**Oʻahu's commercial load is tourism-shaped, not office-shaped**, and counting
heads was hiding that. The visible consequence is the working week: hotels,
restaurants and shops trade seven days, offices do not.

| | Headcount-weighted | **CBECS-weighted** |
|---|---:|---:|
| Weekend ÷ weekday commercial draw | 0.757 | **0.809** |
| Honolulu share, weekday noon | 77.0% | 75.9% |
| Honolulu share, weekend noon | 71.3% | 72.0% |
| Honolulu swing across the year | 34.35 pp | 31.38 pp |
| Share-matrix rank | 2 | **2** |
| Island total preserved | 3×10⁻⁴ MW | **3×10⁻⁴ MW** |

CBECS is **national**; it publishes no state breakout at this detail. Applying
national intensities to a tourism economy understates lodging if anything, so the
correction here is likely conservative.

## Sensitivity: how much do the assumed weights actually matter?

Two layers of the commercial blend are judgement, so both were tested by
rebuilding the shape under deliberately wrong assumptions and measuring how far
the hour-of-day curve moved. The curve is normalised to peak 1.000, so a
deviation of 0.050 means "at its worst hour this sits 5 points of peak away".

**Layer 1 — the mix of building types within a job category** (40/35/25 for
offices, and so on). Fully assumed; no source gives it.

| Scenario | Max deviation | Peak hour |
|---|---:|---:|
| Equal weights everywhere | 0.007 | 13:00 |
| All-largest building per category | 0.055 | 13:00 |
| All-smallest building per category | 0.053 | 13:00 |
| **300 random mixes** | median 0.023, worst **0.062** | **13:00 every time** |

**Layer 2 — the weighting between categories.** Now sourced to CBECS, but tested
anyway for how much a wrong answer would cost.

| Scenario | Max deviation | Peak hour |
|---|---:|---:|
| Health ×3 | 0.070 | 13:00 |
| Hotel + food ×3 | 0.090 | 13:00 |
| Warehouse ×3 | 0.090 | 13:00 |
| Office ×0.5 | 0.024 | 13:00 |
| Schools ×0 | 0.022 | 13:00 |
| **300 random re-weightings, each ×0.33 to ×3** | median 0.044, worst **0.104** | **13:00 every time** |

**Why so insensitive.** The blend averages fifteen curves that are all the same
shape — every commercial building type on Oʻahu wakes around 07:00, peaks in the
early afternoon and goes quiet at night. Averaging things that already agree
cannot produce much disagreement. Contrast the calendar bug, which was a
*structural* error: it moved load onto the wrong days of the week.

**Where this breaks.** The result holds only while nothing in the average sings a
different tune. It would not survive a flat or night-heavy load carrying real
weight — and that is exactly what the military bases are. LODES omits uniformed
personnel, so Pearl Harbor–Hickam, Schofield and Kāneʻohe contribute **nothing**
to this shape despite running around the clock. The exposure is there, not in the
building mix.

## Sensitivity: the three gas turbines, against filed EIA-860 start times

The building-mix test above probes *our own* judgement. This one probes a
judgement that a filed record **contradicts**.

EIA-860's `Operable` sheet carries a field we had overlooked — **`Time from Cold
Shutdown to Full Load`** — now extracted for all 24 units into
`data/processed/oahu_generators.csv` (`cold_start_eia860`, `cold_start_source`).
It corroborates four of our five technology classes and contradicts the fifth:

| Units | Prime mover | Filed cold start | Modelled as | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Schofield S1–S6 (50.4 MW) | IC | 10 min | fast, min-up 1 h | agrees |
| Kalaeloa (220 MW) | CT/CA | 1 h | combined cycle, min-up 6 h | agrees |
| Kahe + Waiau steam (936.3 MW) | ST | 12 h | steam, min-up 8 h | agrees |
| H-POWER (86 MW) | ST | over 12 h | must-run, min-up 24 h | agrees |
| **Waiau W9, W10, CIP1 (215.4 MW)** | **GT** | **12 h** | **peaker, min-up 1 h** | **contradicts** |

Those three are **14.2% of installed capacity** and the only units in the model
that can go zero-to-full inside an hour.

### What the `gt_slow` case changes — and deliberately does not

`Set 9/run_gt_slow.jl` re-specifies **only** the parameters a start time bears on:

| Parameter | Baseline | `gt_slow` |
|---|---:|---:|
| Minimum up time | 1 h | **8 h** |
| Minimum down time | 1 h | **8 h** |
| Start-up cost | $20/MW | **$75/MW** |
| `p_min` | 0 | *unchanged* |
| Ramp rate | 100%/h | *unchanged* |

`p_min` and ramp are held because the filing says nothing about them: a machine
can be slow to light off from cold and still move fast once synchronised. Only
the `:full` rung reads min-up/min-down/start-up cost, so only `:full` is re-run.

### Result

| Metric | Baseline v3 `:full` | `gt_slow` | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Hours with load shed | 18 | 18 | **0** |
| Energy not served (MWh) | 11.67 | 10.29 | −1.39 |
| Total cost ($M) | 689.84 | 689.73 | −0.11 |
| Hours with a line at limit | 5,489 | 5,546 | +57 |

**The headline does not move.** Shed hours are identical. The cost change is
0.016% and the ENS change is 1.4 MWh against ~6.5 million MWh of annual energy —
one to two orders of magnitude *inside* the 1% MIP-gap tolerance every block is
solved to. Read them as solver noise, not as improvement. Slower turbines do
not help; the model simply cannot tell the difference at this rung.

The change is not inert, though: 57 more hours carry a line at its limit, so the
commitment schedule genuinely moved. The schedule just had slack to absorb it.

### What this does NOT clear

The headline Set 9 result is produced at the **`:ramp`** rung, which by
construction reads neither min-up/min-down nor start-up cost. **This sensitivity
cannot vindicate it.** The parameter that result rests on is the hourly **ramp
rate**, and EIA-860 says nothing about ramp rate. It stays an uncited technology
convention.

And note the direction: if those three turbines really are slower than modelled,
the model **overstates** island flexibility, so the ramp shortfall we report is a
**lower bound**. The error runs the conservative way.

Reproduce with:

```bash
julia "Set 9/run_gt_slow.jl"      # writes Set 9/results/set9_gt_slow.csv
```

## Remaining limitations

1. The shapes are **typical-year reference buildings** at Honolulu weather, not
   Oʻahu's metered sector loads. Right diurnal character; not a measurement.
   The building blend is weighted by CBECS **national** energy intensities; the
   within-category mix remains assumed but is measured insensitive (above).
   *Calendar note:* those runs use a reference year whose 1 January is a Sunday,
   two weekdays off 2021. The first build of v3 did not correct for that and read
   weekends as busier than weekdays (1.14×); `PROFILE_DOW_OFFSET = 2` in
   `prepare_hourly_demand.py` now fixes it, and the tables above are the
   corrected run.
2. **Industrial is flat**, and EIA's industrial class here likely includes large
   federal and military accounts that behave more like big commercial customers.
3. **Rooftop PV is allocated by population.** The right source is Hawaiian
   Electric's Locational Value Map — circuit-level, but not available in bulk.
4. **LODES still excludes uniformed military**, so Wahiawa (Schofield) and
   Koolaupoko (Kaneohe) remain under-weighted.
5. **`p_min`, ramp rate and min-up/min-down are uncited technology conventions.**
   EIA-860's cold-start field (above) is the only filed evidence we have on unit
   flexibility; it corroborates four classes, contradicts the combustion
   turbines, and speaks to none of these three parameters directly. Verified
   unit-level operating data is still the highest-value improvement available.
